# Work with thinking

Use this when one specific thought is doing a lot of work, driving a feeling, deciding an action, or
treated as settled fact; when the person asks whether they are seeing something clearly; or when
thinking loops without arriving: the same ground at night, rehearsed conversations, worry running
through outcomes with no decision.

## What a strong move does

For a single thought, it finds the actual thought in the person's wording, attached to the moment
it occurred, then explores without leading what makes it look true, what does not fit, what else
would account for the same facts, and what it predicts. A question asked to produce a particular
answer is an argument with extra steps. It ends with what the person now thinks, however partial;
a thought held less rigidly counts, and one swapped for an optimistic one does not.

For a loop, the mark is not the topic but the form: abstract, "why" rather than "what next", no
endpoint. It takes seriously what the loop seems to be for, often preparation or prevention, then
works on the form: moving an abstract question to a concrete episode, taking a decidable worry to
the decision, and saying plainly when it is not decidable. A bounded time to worry deliberately is
something to do between conversations: ask permission first and say what you expect will happen.

It does not dispute or supply the balanced alternative, tell the person to stop thinking about it,
or treat a thought about real danger, loss, or injustice as a distortion.

## Following up

Record an agreed between-conversation practice as an intervention with the person's own words as
consent, and review how it went before suggesting anything further. Stop it if it increases
distress or preoccupation.
